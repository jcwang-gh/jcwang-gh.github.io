Video Virtual Try-On

I built and trained the animation stage of a video try-on prototype. My model transfers motion from a driving video to the person in a dressed reference image.

Why I start with a still image

When I tested video try-on methods, a new color or print often came through while the cut still matched the old outfit. I designed the pipeline around a single dressed image so I could check the garment’s cut before animating the wearer.

Why I switched to IM-Animation

I considered explicit controls such as a 2D skeleton or an SMPL body mesh. I chose an implicit encoder that learns motion from video so the same reach could adapt to different arm lengths.

I first trained an end-to-end model following X-UniMotion. Even after I reworked the motion retargeter, it struggled to follow the driver’s gestures with the data and compute I had. I switched to IM-Animation so I could train the retargeter before fine-tuning the video generator.

Drawing on both papers, I built separate paths for appearance and motion on the Wan2.2-TI2V-5B backbone. I gave the dressed image its own reference tokens and fed motion and facial expression into the tokens that generate the video.

How I built the training pairs

For IM-Animation I drew on 3.2 hours of real footage from four fashion datasets (UBC-Fashion, TikTokDress, Try-Any-Bench and ViViD). I kept steady shots showing one person’s face, upper body and both hands. Following X-UniMotion’s self-training approach, I took the reference frame and motion from the same shot.

I rendered about 10 hours of paired Mixamo footage in Blender. I gave two characters with different proportions the same action, taking the driving motion from one and the reference still and synchronized target video from the other.

For the final fine-tune I kept 6,782 clips, each with a usable face crop across all 81 frames. Played once at 30 fps, they run for about 5.1 hours.

Source footage and training split

For my earlier experiments I curated 32.9 hours across six real-video datasets. I trained the IM-Animation retargeter on 8,792 clips, including 2,581 real clips and 6,211 Mixamo pairs. I drew the final fine-tuning set from these clips.

Hand and face supervision

In early experiments I used Sapiens joint heatmaps and hand surface normals. The dense maps were expensive to store, and the fine hand and face joints were hard to fit. For IM-Animation I built pose targets from 23 body and 42 hand keypoints detected by DWPose and fed cropped faces to a separate expression encoder. I tested heatmap resolutions and peak widths and reconstructed the training maps on the GPU from a compact cache.

Training and checking motion transfer

I tested an early model by holding the reference fixed and playing the driving clip forward, then backward. The predicted joint positions barely changed on the three frames I checked. I kept this timing check for later runs.

For the model shown here, I started with the IM-Animation authors’ released motion encoder. I trained the retargeter for 40,000 steps on two GPUs, a little over four days. I then fine-tuned Wan for 20,000 steps on one GPU over about three days.
